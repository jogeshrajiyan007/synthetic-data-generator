Yes — you can identify repeat contacts using Episodes alone. The reason to retain Sequence is not to detect whether a customer contacted again; it is to capture the relationship between separate episodes that occur close together.

Think of the hierarchy like this:

Contact → Episode → Sequence → Customer journey

Example

Suppose a customer has these contacts:

Contact	Time	Gap	Episode	Sequence

C1	Mon 09:00	—	E1	S1
C2	Mon 15:00	6h	E1	S1
C3	Tue 10:00	19h	E1	S1
C4	Wed 09:00	23h	E1	S1
C5	Thu 10:00	25h	E2	S1
C6	Fri 09:00	23h	E2	S1
C7	Mon 10:00	73h	E3	S2


With a 24-hour Episode threshold:

C1–C4 → one Episode

C5–C6 → another Episode

C7 → another Episode


So Episodes tell you:

> "This customer had 3 distinct contact episodes."



But with a 48-hour Sequence threshold:

E1 + E2 → Sequence S1

E3 → Sequence S2


So Sequence tells you:

> "Those two separate episodes were part of the same short-term customer interaction journey."




---

Why this matters for your FCR work

Imagine:

Episode 1 → customer contacts → Episode 2

If Episode 2 happens 25 hours later, you probably don't want to treat it identically to an episode that happens 3 months later.

Sequence lets you distinguish:

Pattern A — Immediate repeat

> Contact → Contact → Contact



All within one Episode.

This is useful for identifying failure to resolve within the same interaction period.

Pattern B — Episode-to-episode repeat

> Episode 1 → 25h → Episode 2



This is different.

The first episode ended according to your Episode definition, but the customer returned very soon afterwards.

That can be important evidence for:

unresolved demand

delayed resolution

follow-up requirement

customer having to contact again

operational handoffs

claims/process dependencies


Pattern C — Genuine later re-contact

> Episode 1 → 2 weeks → Episode 2



This may be a completely different customer need.

Sequence helps prevent these very different patterns from being treated as the same thing.


---

But there is an important point for your project

You don't need Sequence just because Episodes exist.

If your immediate objective is simply:

> "Did the customer contact again after the initial contact?"



Then Episode is sufficient.

You can calculate:

Initial Episode
       ↓
Was there another Episode?
       ↓
Repeat Contact

You don't need Sequence for that.


---

Sequence becomes valuable when you want to answer:

> "Are these separate Episodes part of the same ongoing customer demand/journey?"



For example:

Customer
│
├── Sequence 1
│   ├── Episode 1
│   │   ├── Contact
│   │   └── Contact
│   │
│   └── Episode 2
│       └── Contact
│
├── Sequence 2
│   └── Episode 3
│
└── Sequence 3
    ├── Episode 4
    └── Episode 5

That gives you a much richer analytical structure.


---

And this is particularly useful for your FCR model

You could eventually distinguish:

Level 1 — Contact

Was this interaction handled?

↓

Level 2 — Episode

Did the customer continue contacting within the immediate contact window?

↓

Level 3 — Sequence

Did the customer return shortly after the episode ended?

↓

Level 4 — Longer-term repeat

Did the customer come back later?

This is much more informative than simply:

Repeat Contact = Yes/No


---

One thing I would change in your current approach

Don't defend 48 hours by saying:

> "We chose 48 hours because that's the standard."



Instead, your analysis should establish whether the data supports a meaningful separation.

You have already done this for Episode with the threshold sensitivity analysis.

You should do the equivalent for Sequence:

Sequence threshold
        ↓
24h
36h
48h
60h
72h
...
        ↓
How many Episodes are grouped together?
How many Sequences are created?
How many sequences contain multiple episodes?
How long are sequences?

Then you can say something much more defensible:

> "The Sequence threshold was selected based on the observed distribution of gaps between Episodes and sensitivity analysis, rather than being arbitrarily defined."



That is the important part.

So your final MOL structure should be:

Contact → Episode → Sequence

but each level needs a different analytical purpose:

Level	What it represents	Why

Contact	Individual interaction	Raw event
Episode	Continuous/closely connected contact period	Identify immediate repeat behaviour
Sequence	Closely spaced Episodes	Identify an ongoing customer journey
Customer	All interactions over time	Long-term behaviour


And if your only deliverable were FCR / repeat contact, I would keep the model simpler and use Episodes. Sequence earns its place when you need to understand the journey between Episodes, not merely whether a repeat happened.
